# What You Hate Isn't Management — It's Being Asked to Pretend

Eight-thirty on a Monday morning, an engineer opens their laptop.
They have a bug to fix. This bug can only be fixed by them — not because they're the best, but because they're the only person on the team who has read the inherited code. Fixing it will take maybe two or three hours of focused time.
They don't have two or three hours.
There are four meetings on the calendar. The first is standup — fifteen minutes, except it always runs to forty, because someone needs to describe in detail, in front of the manager, what they did yesterday. The second is a cross-team sync, an hour, in which their role is to listen. The third is sprint planning, another hour. The fourth is an OKR alignment, crammed in at quarter's end.
Before the first meeting starts, they see a message on Slack. A colleague @'d them: a comment in yesterday's pull request used the phrase "sanity check" — "some people might find that term not inclusive toward those with mental health conditions; suggest changing it to 'validity check.'"
They stare at the message for five seconds.
They don't think "sanity check" is a malicious term. They don't think the colleague who flagged it is a bad person. They understand that language can cause harm, and they're willing to mind their wording. But at the same time they realize one thing: **their day will be spent proving they're working, proving they're in meetings, proving their word choices are sufficiently correct — and not on the one bug only they can fix.**
Three things that look entirely unrelated: an approval workflow, a wording suggestion, a calendar packed full. But sitting at their desk, what they feel is the same thing —
Someone is managing them. But no one is helping them.

## Two Revolutions With Completely Different Starting Points

To understand how management and political correctness turned from tools into cages, it helps to return to the moment they were invented. Because their origins share one feature: **both began as a rebellion against unconstrained power.**

### Weber's Ideal: Replacing Caprice With Rules

In the 1920s, the German sociologist Max Weber proposed the ideal type of bureaucracy. Today "bureaucracy" is a slur, but the problem Weber designed it to solve was real and urgent.
Before Weber, organizations ran mainly on two kinds of authority: **traditional authority** ("because it's always been this way") and **charismatic authority** ("because the boss said so"). This meant your promotion depended on your personal relationship with the boss, your salary depended on the boss's mood that day, your livelihood depended on whether you belonged to the right family or clique.
Weber's alternative was rational-legal authority. Its core is six principles: hierarchy, formal rules, division of labor, impersonality, career orientation, and formal selection. In plain terms: rules apply equally to everyone, promotion is based on competence not relationships, decisions are documented not improvised.
At the time this was a liberation. For a worker tormented by a boss's arbitrary decisions, a clear set of rules everyone must follow was tremendous good news. Weber's bureaucracy was a constraint on **personal power** — it transferred power out of people's hands and into the hands of rules.
But Weber himself saw the danger. He called bureaucracy's terminal form the "iron cage" — a system based on efficiency, rational calculation, and control that traps the individual inside it. Rules were meant to be tools, but rules tend to self-replicate. In 1955 the British historian Parkinson discovered a regularity nearly at the level of a physical law: **officials tend to multiply subordinates, not rivals.** A department head expands their influence not by solving problems but by hiring more people — even if those people have little real work.
From "using rules to constrain the boss" to "rules themselves becoming the new boss" — this slide requires no conspiracy, only time and rational people's rational responses.

### The Origins of PC: Using Language to Pry at Power

The history of "political correctness" is far longer, and far more twisted, than most people assume.
The term can be traced back to a 1930s Marxist-Leninist context — there it described doctrinaire obedience to totalitarian ideology: Is your position aligned with the party line? Are you politically "correct"? In the USSR and Nazi Germany, this was a grave question with consequences.
But it wasn't totalitarians who brought PC into the English-speaking world; it was the **New Left of the 1970s–80s**. They borrowed the term from English translations of Chinese Cultural Revolution texts — but their usage was **self-mocking**. When a feminist said in a meeting "That's not politically correct," she carried a knowing irony: I know this sounds doctrinaire, but think about the social power structure behind the word you just used.
This starting point had value. If a linguistic habit long disadvantages a group (say, using "he" to refer to everyone, or using racial slurs for colleagues), pointing out the power structure in that habit isn't dogma — it's necessary critique. Language really can cause harm; marginalized groups really do endure linguistic violence over long stretches.
The turn in the 1990s came from outside: conservatives launched a culture-war offensive against the New Left. PC went from a tool of self-examination inside the left to an attack label of the right. *The Atlantic*, *The New York Times*, campus debates — "political correctness" suddenly became a target everyone took aim at. Ironically, this external attack actually **hardened** PC's defensive lines: when a practice is attacked by enemies, its supporters tend to defend it more firmly, including defending its excessive extensions.
From "using language to examine power structures" to "linguistic policing itself becoming a power structure" — this slide, too, requires no conspiracy. It needs only an external threat (the conservative attack) and an internal tendency (a group calcifying under opposition).

### From Constraining Power to Becoming Power

Weber's bureaucracy constrained the boss's caprice. PC constrained the majority's linguistic violence. Both took "limiting unconstrained power" as their starting point.
But both went through the same reversal: **a tool for constraining power, given enough time, itself became a new and hard-to-challenge power.**
Bureaucratic rules no longer serve efficiency but the perpetuation of the rules themselves. You cannot question the existence of a process — because the existence of the process is its own justification.
PC norms no longer serve fairness but the status of the norms themselves. You cannot question the effect of a guideline — because questioning is itself taken as evidence that you don't support fairness.
Graeber had a sharp observation here: neoliberalism promised to reduce bureaucracy through privatization and deregulation. The result? Bureaucracy not only didn't shrink, it exploded — only it shifted from government bureaucracy to corporate bureaucracy. You no longer queue at a government window, but you spend far more time than your parents' generation circling with insurance-company customer service, filling out a SaaS product's compliance forms, replying to the HR system's automated questionnaires.
Management went from "constraining power" to "exercising power." PC went from "challenging power" to "defending power." The processes differed, but the landing point is the same: **a system that demands you prove your own compliance, where compliance is defined by the system itself.**
Understand this history and you understand why saying "I hate management" and "I hate PC" is not opposing order or fairness — you're opposing one particular degenerate form. Management's degenerate form is infinitely self-replicating rules. PC's degenerate form is an unquestionable posture of correctness. Their shared degenerate form is: **the tool became the goal.**

## A Formula

Before going into specific mechanisms, we need a frame.
The political scientist James C. Scott proposed a concept in *Seeing Like a State* called **legibility**. His core argument: to govern, a state must simplify complex reality into a readable format. Old-growth forests were remade into orderly rows of plantation — convenient for calculating timber yield, but it killed biodiversity. Irregular villages were remade into gridded cities — convenient for taxation and census, but it destroyed community bonds.
**Management is the legibility project inside an organization.** It simplifies complex human activity — creativity, judgment, collaboration, conflict, intuition — into signals that fit in a table: KPI, OKR, sprint velocity, performance rating. These metrics are not reality; they're a low-resolution map of reality. But to the organization's "state" (the management layer), they are the only operable thing.
**PC is the legibility project in social interaction.** It simplifies complex human relations — respect, prejudice, goodwill, harm, cultural difference, power inequality — into auditable signals: word choice, stated positions, behavioral codes. These signals are not goodwill itself; they're proxy metrics for goodwill. But to society's "state" (institutions, media, HR), they are the only enforceable thing.
This is why the two things you hate feel so alike: **they are two expressions of the same operation — simplifying complex human judgment into an auditable signal system.**
The British economist Charles Goodhart proposed in 1975 a law later named after him: **when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.**
This law's manifestation in management hardly needs explaining. When "lines of code" becomes a productivity metric, engineers start writing redundant code. When "tickets closed" becomes a performance metric, complex problems get split into ten simple tickets. When OKR completion rate affects bonuses, people start setting deliberately conservative goals — then "exceed" them.
In the PC domain, the same degeneration occurs. When "using inclusive language" becomes a social metric, people learn to say the right words without truly understanding. When "no complaints" becomes an organizational metric, problems aren't solved, they're silenced. When "diversity numbers" becomes a compliance goal, the numbers get hit while the structural problem remains untouched.
Stanford's Leonardo Braghieri found in a carefully designed experiment: **there is a systematic divergence between people's public expression and their private beliefs — and audiences only partly perceive that divergence.** In other words, everyone is to some degree "performing correctness," and the people around them can only dimly sense the performance. Cato Institute's 2017 survey data was more direct: **71% of Americans believe political correctness has suppressed things they wanted to say.**
Graeber pushed this phenomenon to its logical endpoint. He pointed out that a large share of work in the modern economy — what he called bullshit jobs — is essentially a product of Goodhart's law: they exist not because anyone needs their output, but because the organization needs them to maintain its own legibility. His taxonomy includes five kinds: flunkies (who make others look important), goons (proxy aggressive force), duct tapers (who patch problems that shouldn't exist), box tickers (who manufacture the illusion of compliance), and taskmasters (who manage others doing meaningless work).
Stack these three frames together — Scott's legibility, Goodhart's law, Graeber's bullshit jobs — and you get a self-reinforcing degeneration formula:

> **Complex judgment → simplified into auditable signal → signal becomes the goal → cost of faking trends to zero → signal loses meaning → system doubles down on auditing → more signal, less substance**

This is not a linear process. It's a positive feedback loop. And it works on management and PC in almost exactly the same way.

## Four Suffocation Mechanisms

If legibility is the cause and Goodhart's law is the course of the disease, then four concrete transmission mechanisms are what make you feel the suffocation every day. They operate in management and political correctness in almost symmetrical ways.

### Mechanism One: Signal Inflation

Evolutionary biology has a theory called costly signaling: for a signal to be credible, the cost of faking it must be high enough.
The peacock's tail is a good example. The bigger and more splendid the tail, the greater the disadvantage when fleeing predators — precisely because of this, a large tail is a credible signal of "my genes really are good." If every peacock, good genes or bad, could grow an equally splendid tail, the tail would carry no information.
This principle applies directly to management and PC.
Before management appeared, whether a person was competent at work carried a high signal cost — you actually had to produce results, and results take time, skill, and luck. But when the management system introduced low-cost substitute signals — weekly reports, OKR alignments, Jira status updates — the cost of faking plummeted. You don't need to actually fix the bug; you just need to say at standup that you're working on it, move a card on Jira, write a paragraph of progress in the weekly report.
Political correctness is the same. Genuine goodwill and respect are a costly signal — it requires you to spend time understanding others' situations, to take on risk at key moments to speak for the weaker group, to choose fairness when interests conflict. These all have real cost. But when the PC system introduced low-cost substitute signals — using correct pronouns in emails, retweeting correct positions on social media, attending sensitivity training — you can obtain the same social return at extremely low cost.
Economics has a perfect analogy: **inflation**.
When the central bank prints too much money, the purchasing power of each note drops. When the system allows too many low-cost signals, the information content of each signal drops. You attended diversity training — does that show you respect diversity, or merely that you were afraid not to attend? You write OKR updates every week — does that show you're doing valuable work, or merely that you can fill out a form?
When signal cost approaches zero, the signal degenerates into noise. An *Aeon* essay on virtue signaling precisely described the psychological aftermath of this degeneration: "When people pay no meaningful price for their positions, they activate the alarm systems evolution installed in our brains over millions of years — the ones meant to protect us from fair-weather friends and moral hypocrites."
This is why the emotion you feel facing a management meeting and a diversity statement is so similar — that implicit, hard-to-name sense of falseness. Your intuition is telling you: the signals here carry no information. Everyone is broadcasting noise.

### Mechanism Two: Compliance Theater

The security expert Bruce Schneier coined a term, security theater, for measures that look like they make you safer but in practice have no effect.
The classic example is the TSA requiring you to take off your shoes at security. In 2001 someone tried to hide a bomb in their shoe (and failed); for twenty-some years since, millions of people a day take off their shoes at airports worldwide. Schneier's assessment is blunt: "I'm quite sure this has never caught anyone. If you check shoes, they put the bomb somewhere else."
But the TSA won't cancel the shoe policy. Because the function of taking off shoes isn't security — it's **visibility**. It makes passengers feel "someone is protecting me," lets politicians say "we're doing something." It's a product of legibility: real security measures (intelligence networks, data analysis, plainclothes police) are invisible, and therefore politically equivalent to not existing.
Management has its own security theater.
The **weekly report** isn't for conveying information — if it were, you wouldn't need a fixed format and fixed-cadence ritual; you'd just send a message when needed. The weekly report is to make "management is happening" visible. The **OKR review** isn't for calibrating goals — real goal calibration happens in a five-minute hallway conversation, not in a three-hour end-of-quarter meeting. The OKR review is to make "the organization has direction" visible. **360 feedback** isn't to help you grow — Gallup's research found managers account for at least 70% of the variance in employee engagement, but 360 feedback scatters attention in all directions, making the real problem (usually the manager) invisible.
PC has its own security theater too.
The **diversity statement** isn't to promote diversity — if it were, what you'd need is to change hiring processes and promotion criteria, not have every candidate write an essay about how much they value diversity. The diversity statement is to make "we value inclusion" visible. **Sensitivity training** isn't to change behavior — research from Miami Herbert Business School published in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* found that PC behavior leads to cognitive depletion; people are more prone to anger or withdrawal toward their spouse after getting home. The study was titled "Walking on Eggshells."
The curtain over walking on eggshells is drawn open so the neighbors can see you sweeping. But you're not really sweeping.
The most dangerous thing about compliance theater isn't that it wastes time — though it does. The most dangerous thing is that it manufactures **false confidence**. When every dashboard shows green, leadership assumes all is well. But green only means "the legibility metric was satisfied," not "the actual problem was solved." The security-theater report shows all compliance checks passed, but the security team knows the critical vulnerability has been open for months.

### Mechanism Three: Adverse Selection

In a system that rewards signals rather than substance, who wins?
The answer is simple: the people who are good at broadcasting signals.
Insurance markets have a classic concept called adverse selection: when buyers of insurance know their own risk level better than sellers, the people most likely to buy insurance are precisely the highest-risk ones — which steadily degrades the quality of the insurance pool.
Management systems have exactly the same dynamic. When promotion depends on visibility rather than competence, the people best at "managing up" get promoted. Their skill is turning a three-line code change into a captivating report, turning an ordinary meeting into a stage for showcasing their leadership. Meanwhile the people who actually solve problems — the ones who stare at the screen for two hours and then fix a critical bug by changing three lines — are invisible on the legibility metrics.
JP Morgan's CEO Jamie Dimon put it bluntly in a talk at Stanford's business school: "Bureaucracy will kill you. It chases away good people. It chases away innovation. It turns the person in the next cubicle into a competitor instead of a collaborator."
Graeber's bullshit-jobs taxonomy includes a kind called taskmasters — people who manage others doing meaningless work. Taskmasters aren't bad people. They're rational people's rational response in an adverse-selection system: if the system rewards "looking like you're managing" over "actual output," then the most rational career strategy is to become a manager — even if what you manage is itself meaningless.
Adverse selection in the PC domain exists too. When "the correct posture" is more easily seen and rewarded than "sincere action," the people best at occupying the moral high ground gain the floor — not necessarily the people who most sincerely care about fairness. People who care about fairness may quietly change their own behavior, practicing respect in concrete daily interactions, but these are invisible in a legibility system. By contrast, writing an immaculately worded justice declaration on social media, or being first to point out someone's improper wording in a meeting, is highly visible.
The result is a double adverse selection: the management layer is occupied by people good at broadcasting signals, the discourse arena is dominated by people good at claiming the high ground. The people who actually do things and the people who are actually kind are both pushed to the margins.

### Mechanism Four: The Spiral of Silence

In 1974, the German communications scholar Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann proposed the spiral of silence theory. The core observation is simple: humans have a built-in "quasi-statistical organ" constantly scanning the surrounding opinion climate. When you sense your view belongs to the minority, you choose silence for fear of social isolation. The more people fall silent, the stronger the majority's voice appears. The stronger the majority, the more people choose silence. This is a positive feedback loop.
In the pre-internet era, the speed of this loop was limited by the frequency of face-to-face interaction. But social media did not break the spiral of silence the way people hoped — quite the opposite. A 2014 Pew Research Center study found: **people's willingness to discuss sensitive political topics on social media is even lower than face-to-face.** The reason may be that digitization dramatically expanded the scope of "being seen" — say something off-message face-to-face and only a few people hear it; say the same thing on Twitter and it might be screenshotted and spread to tens of thousands.
The spiral of silence in management manifests as: when you see a colleague flagged as "not a team player" for questioning a process, you learn to shut your mouth. When you see the person who raised a dissenting opinion docked points in performance review, you learn to go along. Every silent person makes the remaining voices more uniform, and the more uniform voices make more people choose silence. Eventually, the organization's "consensus" may represent only a minority's true thoughts — but no one knows, because everyone assumes they are the minority.
The spiral of silence in the PC domain is even more pronounced. Braghieri's Stanford experiment directly quantified it: there's a systematic divergence between public expression and private belief, and audiences only partly perceive it. This means each person calibrates their behavior against others' public expression (rather than true belief) — and others' public expression has itself already been distorted by social pressure. It's a distortion loop built on top of distorted signals.
The communications scholar Robert Simpson found a subtler dynamic: speech restriction not only creates a "chilling effect" (silencing moderates), it sometimes simultaneously creates a "heating effect" (making extremists more extreme). Both effects can occur at once in the same system — the moderate, nuanced voices vanish, and what remains is either completely silent people or radicalized people. The quality of public discourse doesn't decline linearly; it collapses from the middle.

### The Synergy of the Four Mechanisms

These four mechanisms don't operate independently. They feed each other, forming a self-reinforcing closed loop:
**Signal inflation** drowns real information in noise → **compliance theater** fills the noise with ritualized performance → **adverse selection** elevates the people best at manufacturing noise into decision-making positions → they make more rules that produce noise → the **spiral of silence** wipes out the last batch of people who speak the truth → back to signal inflation.
This is the source of that hard-to-articulate but omnipresent sense of suffocation. It's not caused by a bad boss or a bad rule — it's the systematic output of a structural loop.
And the most poisonous trait of this loop is: **it is rational for every individual participant.** No one needs malice. Everyone is making the choice most advantageous to themselves — fill out the form, say the correct thing, don't challenge the process, don't offend anyone — and the sum of these individual rational choices is the collectively irrational suffocation.

## Those Places That "Don't Manage"

If the degenerate forms of management and PC suffocate people, then what is an organization with none of them like? The answer is more complex than you'd think.

### Valve: The Price of Freedom

The game company Valve has a famous employee handbook. One line in it has been cited by countless management articles: "At Valve, the proportion of self-directed project time is 100%." No managers, no reporting chain, no one assigns tasks. Employees "vote with their feet" — their desks have wheels and can be rolled next to any project they want to join.
This sounds like utopia. But a 2023 PC Gamer deep-dive revealed another side: former employees pointed out that Valve's flat structure led to serious lack of diversity and an extremely poor delivery record. No formal power structure doesn't mean no power — it just means power becomes implicit. Whose project can attract people depends on who has clout, and the way clout accumulates is itself biased. Valve killed formal management, but informal politics thrived more than before.
This isn't to say Valve's model failed. Valve is one of the most profitable game companies per capita in the world; the Steam platform dominates PC game distribution. But it proves one thing: **eliminating formal management doesn't equal eliminating the power problem — sometimes it just makes the power problem invisible.**

### The Linux Kernel: A Culture of Speaking Plainly

If Valve represents the extreme of "no management," the Linux kernel community represents another possibility: there is management, but the manager is allowed — even expected — to speak the truth.
Linus Torvalds is known for a code-review style that is direct to the point of being abrasive. He'll tell a contributor on the public mailing list "this code is garbage" — not because he enjoys humiliating people, but because in a kernel project that runs on billions of devices worldwide, compromising on quality has real consequences. Under Torvalds's governance, the Linux kernel's core maintainers have stayed in their roles for over 30 years on average — nearly unheard of in open-source communities.
Contrast this with corporate management styles: managers are trained to give feedback in a way that "diplomatically says nothing" — "this is an interesting direction, but maybe we could consider some other options?" This phrasing makes everyone feel good, but no one knows what's actually wrong with their code. Signal cost is zero, information transmission is zero.
Torvalds's model has clear costs: in 2018 he acknowledged his communication style had harmed some contributors, and he took a brief leave to reflect. But the key distinction is: in the Linux kernel community, **the cost of speaking the truth is borne by the speaker** (Torvalds takes the criticism and social pressure), not by the listener (a contributor isn't fired or socially killed for receiving a harsh review). In corporate management and PC systems, the cost of speaking the truth is also borne by the speaker, but the form of the cost is punishment — performance dings, social isolation, career risk.

### Genuinely Useful Work Doesn't Need to Prove Itself Useful

Studying bullshit jobs, Graeber found a counterintuitive pattern: **genuinely useful work (nurses, teachers, firefighters, plumbers) tends to be the least "managed."** No one asks a firefighter to write a weekly report to prove they put out fires. No one makes a plumber attend OKR reviews to align their quarterly goals.
But this freedom is being eroded. Nurses report spending 80% of their time on paperwork — records, compliance forms, all kinds of documents insurers demand — rather than caring for patients. Teachers spend more and more time coping with standardized tests and administrative reports, and less and less time teaching.
These cases aren't arguing "management is bad" or "anarchism is the answer." An organization with no coordination mechanism at all will collapse — that's common sense. The question is never "order or no order." The question is: **whose order? for whose benefit? at what cost?**
When order serves the people doing the work — helping them coordinate, reducing friction, getting resources — it is a tool. When order starts to serve itself — demanding people prove they're following the order, while following the order itself consumes the energy for doing the work — it turns from a tool into a cage.
Genuinely useful work doesn't need to prove itself useful. Genuine goodwill doesn't need to be audited. Once you need to prove it, the proof has already replaced the thing it was meant to prove.

## Three Diagnostic Questions

By now you might be thinking: if all management and all PC norms can degenerate into cages, how do I tell which are useful coordination and which are self-serving control?
Here are three questions. Not answers — diagnostic tools.
**Question one: whose cost does this rule eliminate?**
If a rule lowers the friction for the people actually doing the work — making it easier for engineers to find the information they need, sparing minority colleagues from enduring linguistic violence at every meeting — it's a tool.
If a rule lowers the cost for the rule-makers or enforcers — making it easier for managers to write good-looking reports, making it easier for HR to cope with compliance audits — while shifting the cost onto the people actually doing the work (filling out forms, attending meetings, vetting their wording), it's control.
The method of judgment is simple: **see where the cost flows.**
**Question two: if no one were watching, would you still do it this way?**
If you flag a problem in code review because you genuinely believe code quality matters, not because you want to display your "technical leadership" in performance review — that's doing the work.
If you attend diversity training because you genuinely want to understand a colleague's situation, not because skipping it gets you flagged — that's goodwill.
But if, with the audience removed (boss, HR, social media), you wouldn't do the same thing — then you're performing compliance. Performing compliance isn't your fault. It's the inevitable output of a system that reduced signal cost to zero. But recognizing this is the first step toward getting out.
**Question three: what behavior does this system reward?**
The most effective way to read a system isn't to look at what it claims to pursue, but at what it actually rewards.
If the system rewards "did something" — solved a customer problem, wrote good code, helped a struggling colleague — it rewards substance.
If the system rewards "proved you did something" — wrote a slick weekly report, said the correct thing, attended the correct meeting — Goodhart has already won. The gap between metric and reality is exactly the soil in which bullshit jobs grow.

That engineer sitting at their desk on Monday morning — squeezed between five layers of approval, a "not inclusive" label, and a calendar full of sync meetings — what do they need?
Not better management. Not more correct wording. Not more process training or finer behavioral codes.
They need **to be trusted to make judgments**.
Trust is not measurable. Trust is not auditable. Trust cannot be written into an OKR or a compliance checklist. Precisely for this reason, it can barely exist in a system built around legibility.
But trust is the thing that makes an organization truly run — the thing that keeps good people, gets bad code fixed, lets people of different backgrounds genuinely collaborate rather than merely pretend to.
What you hate has never been being coordinated. What you hate is being asked to pretend.
