# Cognitive Precocity: Why Some People Have Profound Insight So Young

In autumn 2008, a student who had just been admitted to Peking University from Huizhou was working on a math problem.
Not calculus, not advanced math. It was a game-theory problem about his own life.
His name was Justin Sun, 18, a freshman in PKU's Chinese department. His grades were upper-middle—and in PKU's Chinese department, packed with elites, "upper-middle" means you'll never rank first in your year. He needed to be first in his year. Not out of perfectionism, but because he'd done the arithmetic: ranking first meant top recommendation letters, meant admission to Ivy League schools, meant a path into a completely different information environment.
His solution baffled his peers: at the end of freshman year, he transferred down into the history department.
Ranking upper-middle in PKU's Chinese department is worse than ranking first in the history department. A champion on a less competitive track is worth more than an upper-middle player on a more competitive one. This is the logic Sun Bin taught Tian Ji over two thousand years ago—pit your worst horse against the opponent's best, your best against their middling one. Don't grind within the rules; jump out of the rules and choose your track.
For an 18-year-old to think this far is already unusual. Even more unusual is that he actually did it. In a culture where "transferring down" is seen as humiliating and "switching majors" as giving up, he treated it as an arbitrage move.
The result was as he'd calculated. He graduated first in his year from PKU's history department, won admission to five Ivy League schools, and chose the University of Pennsylvania. There, he used his tuition money to buy 1,000 bitcoins—at 6 cents each at the time. Two years later, he became one of the earliest team members in China for the blockchain company Ripple. Later, at 27, he founded TRON, whose ICO raised $70 million within 53 seconds. By 2025, at 35, he was on Forbes' global billionaires list with a fortune of $8.5 billion.
This is a story about "winning." But what this essay wants to ask is not "how did he win"—too many people have already answered that.
What this essay wants to ask is a deeper question: **Why can some people, so young, possess such profound insight?**
And a question fewer people pursue: **Is this kind of profundity the kind of profundity we think it is?**

## Cognitive Precocity Is Not This Era's Invention

Cognitive precocity is an ancient phenomenon; it just wears different clothes in different eras.
In 334 BC, a 22-year-old king of Macedon led forty thousand men across the Hellespont and invaded one of the most powerful empires in human history—Persia. That man was Alexander the Great. Over the next eight years, he fought all the way to the Indus valley, winning all 20 major battles, building a vast empire from Greece to India. The territory he controlled by 30 exceeded that of any previous monarch.
Beginning to conquer the world at 22—what kind of cognitive ability does that require? Not just military genius—though that's beyond doubt—but more importantly a strategic judgment: knowing when to concentrate forces, when to divide them, when to confront the enemy head-on, when to flank around. And that judgment he already possessed at 22.
Fast-forward two thousand three hundred years. In 2004, a 19-year-old Mark Zuckerberg wrote Facebook in a Harvard dorm. In 2013, a 19-year-old Vitalik Buterin published the Ethereum white paper—a conception of a decentralized computing platform almost no one could understand at the time. Buterin encountered bitcoin at 17; while others were still debating "is this thing a Ponzi scheme," he was already thinking about "what are the limitations of bitcoin's architecture, and how to solve them with a more general platform." Two years later Ethereum went live, reaching a market cap of hundreds of billions by 2025.
Justin Sun in the same period, at 23, was doing something similar: when the vast majority of people didn't even know what blockchain was, he was already in Ripple's Silicon Valley office, taking part firsthand in the early building of the field.
The past mainstream explanations for this phenomenon were two: talent and luck.
The "talent" theory says: these people are simply born smarter than others. Vitalik Buterin's IQ is estimated at around 160, in the extreme 0.003% of the population. But the talent theory has a fatal problem—it can't explain why the vast majority of equally high-IQ people did not display this kind of insight at 23. There are hundreds of thousands of people worldwide with IQ over 160, but only one wrote the Ethereum white paper at 19.
The "luck" theory says: they happened to be in the right place at the right time. Luck is of course real, but it likewise can't explain everything—many people were in the same time and place, so why did only a tiny few make that key decision?
Cognitive scientist K. Anders Ericsson proposed a more explanatory framework in 1993: expert performance is not the product of talent but of **deliberate practice**. His core finding was that even in fields thought to depend most on talent, individual differences are mainly explained by the quantity and quality of deliberate practice. But Ericsson's theory was later simplified into the "10,000-hour rule"—a misreading. He clarified repeatedly: what matters is not the absolute length of time but the structure of practice—especially **deliberately escalating difficulty** and **an immediate feedback loop**.
This offers a new angle on cognitive precocity: it's not that these people practiced ten thousand hours, but that in far less than ten thousand hours they achieved a higher density of effective cognitive iterations.
A more intuitive analogy: two people learn to swim. One goes to the pool once a week, swims, then goes home and waits for next week. The other swims daily in rapids, nearly drowning each time, with a coach on shore telling him where he went wrong. After a year, the second person's level will far exceed the first's, even if their total practice time is similar.
The difference isn't in time, it's in density. The difference isn't in how many things you experienced, but how much cognition you compressed out of each one.

## The Real Formula for Cognitive Depth

We hold a deeply rooted intuition about "cognitive depth": that it's a product of time. A person has to live long enough, experience enough, take enough hits to see through some things. This intuition is right most of the time. But it can't explain Justin Sun.
In 2013, the 23-year-old Justin Sun was still doing a master's at Penn. He used his tuition and living expenses to buy 1,000 bitcoins at an average of 6 cents each. In the same period, he was also heavily invested in Tesla, earning over 4x returns. Two years later he joined Ripple's China team. Two more years, at 27, he founded TRON, whose ICO sold 500 million tokens within 53 seconds of launch on Binance, raising about $70 million. By early 2018, TRON's market cap broke $10 billion.
What were his peers doing? The vast majority of 23-year-olds were still agonizing over grad school vs. jobs, or just figuring out how to use a credit card.
This isn't a question of IQ. China produces millions of college graduates each year, including no shortage of high-IQ people. But the vast majority of them, even at 40, won't display the judgment Justin Sun had at 23—buying bitcoin with his living expenses in an era when almost no one had heard of it; seeing its value as financial infrastructure when blockchain was still a geek toy.
The problem isn't "smart or not," it's a deeper variable: **information compression rate**.
If you wrote cognitive depth as an equation, it's not:

> Cognitive depth = Age × Experience

but closer to:

> Cognitive depth = Information density × Feedback frequency × Pattern-recognition ability

Age in this equation is only a weak variable. It affects "how much time you have to accumulate," but doesn't determine "how much cognition you can compress per unit of time."
Break down Justin Sun's case, and all three variables were pushed to extreme values:
**Information density.** The double elite circles of PKU and Penn exposed him at 23 to the world's most cutting-edge technology and financial information. While ordinary people slowly accumulate common knowledge within their peer circle, he was already sitting in Ripple's Silicon Valley office, discussing the future of decentralized clearing with the earliest people in the blockchain industry. Information density doesn't grow linearly—enter a higher-dimensional circle and the information supply leaps exponentially.
**Feedback frequency.** Justin Sun is not a person who "wants to be sure before acting." He's a person who "acts first and thinks later"—and acts extremely fast. At PKU he founded *Weekly Review* to comment on campus affairs, with posts breaking a million views; at Penn he traded stocks and coins with his tuition; he started a company immediately after returning to China. Every action brought real market feedback, and every feedback corrected and strengthened his cognitive model. While others were still learning "investment principles" from textbooks, he had completed dozens of cognitive iteration cycles with real money.
**Pattern recognition.** This may be the hardest variable to quantify. Sun, ranking upper-middle in PKU's Chinese department, made a decision a 21-year-old rarely makes: transfer down into the history department. His logic was simple—ranking first on a weaker track is more useful than ranking middle on a strong one. This is classic "Tian Ji racing horses" thinking, essentially a game-theoretic intuition: don't grind within the rules, jump out and choose your track. His later choices of ICO timing and the marketing play of the Buffett lunch were all variations of the same pattern recognition.

### The Assumption Hidden in the Formula

But this formula has an overlooked premise: what direction of cognition is it optimizing?
Look closely at the combination of these three variables—high information density brings "opportunity recognition," high feedback frequency brings "strategy iteration," strong pattern recognition brings "game-theoretic advantage." They all point to the same thing: **winning**.
Winning better grades, winning investment returns, winning market share, winning attention.
This is different from another kind of cognitive depth—"understanding." Understanding why the world is the way it is, understanding what a decision means for others, understanding that a short-term win may carry a long-term cost. This kind of cognition can't be gained by accelerating information intake and shortening feedback cycles, because what it needs isn't more data points, but a longer time span to observe consequences.
Justin Sun once said something himself: "PR is the beating heart; you have to jolt it now and then, or it dies. Eating dirt is ugly, but there's no other way."
This statement is itself an excellent cognitive sample. He knew clearly what he was doing, and knew clearly how the outside world saw it—that's extremely high-level game-theoretic cognition. But the judgment "eating dirt is ugly, no other way" itself shows that in his objective function, the weight of "winning" far exceeds "being right."
A blockchain developer once evaluated TRON: "Success is 30% technology, 70% marketing." The same ratio applies to describing Justin Sun's cognitive structure—his cognitive depth is real, but its distribution is uneven, heavily skewed toward the game-theoretic end.
This is the most worthwhile part of cognitive precocity to discuss: not whether it exists, but **which dimension it optimizes**.

## Four Cognitive Accelerators

If cognitive precocity does exist, what are its acceleration mechanisms? Break down the people who display superhuman cognition young, and you can identify four recurring "cognitive accelerators."

### Accelerator One: Circle Leaps

Ordinary people accumulate cognition like climbing stairs: peer circle → entry-level workplace circle → industry circle → executive circle. Each layer takes years or even more than a decade. The cognitively precocious take a path more like an elevator—they actively jump into information fields far above their current level, substituting environmental density for time length.
Justin Sun's circle-leap path is extremely clear: PKU Chinese department → first in year in PKU history department → University of Pennsylvania → Ripple Silicon Valley team → first cohort student of Jack Ma's Hupan University → advisor to a Trump crypto project. Each step was not natural promotion, but an active jump.
The key here isn't "entering an elite school" itself—tens of thousands enter elite schools each year—but what he did in each circle. At PKU, he didn't settle into studying; he founded *Weekly Review* to intervene in campus public affairs, with posts breaking a million reads. At Penn, he didn't focus on academics; he invested his tuition in Tesla and bitcoin while founding the new-media platform "North America Three," building a network of Chinese students in North America. At Ripple, he wasn't a quiet employee; he quickly became the core promoter for the China region.
Sociologist Mark Granovetter proposed "the strength of weak ties" in 1973—cross-circle weak connections often bring more new information than strong ties within a circle. Justin Sun's strategy can be seen as an extreme practice of this theory: he didn't wait for weak ties to appear naturally, he actively manufactured cross-circle jumps, rapidly building connections and extracting information in each new circle.
But this accelerator has a hidden cost: you stay in each circle for a very short time, with no time to build deep relationships and deep understanding. What you gain is the circle's "information essence," not its "operating logic." It's like someone who quickly skims the tables of contents and abstracts of ten books—he can talk a good game at dinner, but he hasn't necessarily truly read any of them.

### Accelerator Two: The Action-Feedback Flywheel

Ericsson's true core point wasn't "practice 10,000 hours and you're set," but: **what matters isn't the length of practice but its structure—especially the quality and frequency of feedback**.
Justin Sun's action mode naturally fits this principle. He's not a thinker, he's a doer—and an extremely high-frequency doer.
Consider a contrast: an ordinary finance graduate might spend two years as a junior analyst at a brokerage, accumulating investment cognition by reading reports and writing models. Justin Sun, in the same two years, operated directly in the market with his own money—buying Tesla, buying bitcoin, buying Chinese concept stocks. The former's feedback is "your boss said your report was well-written"; the latter's is "your account is up 20x" or "you've blown through your living expenses." The density and authenticity of these two kinds of feedback are not in the same league.
His later ventures followed the same pattern. The 2017 TRON ICO, sold out in 53 seconds, raised $70 million—not just a financing event, but a huge cognitive feedback signal: the market was willing to buy "Justin Sun + blockchain narrative," and to this degree. The 2019 Buffett lunch bought for $4.56 million—by analysis, this investment brought TRON roughly $250 million in brand value uplift, a return over 50x. These numbers aren't just business results; they're calibration signals for cognition: what works, what doesn't, what the market's pricing logic is.
The limitation of this flywheel: it only works in fields with clear market feedback. Investment has prices, ventures have market caps, marketing has traffic—all games you can quickly score. But in fields needing long-cycle feedback—the deep cultivation of relationships, long-term organization-building, or evaluating the consequences of moral judgments—this flywheel fails, because the feedback delay may be years or even decades.

### Accelerator Three: Narrative Ability

Cognition isn't only the product of "input," it's also the product of "output." When you need to organize a vague cognition into a clear narrative—whether writing an article, pitching investors, or packaging a marketing story—the output process itself forces your cognition to become more precise and more structured.
Justin Sun's narrative ability is the most prominent of all his skills, and the most underestimated.
At 17, he won first prize in the 9th New Concept Writing Competition—a contest that also produced Han Han and Guo Jingming. After entering PKU, he published articles in *Mengya* magazine, breaking a million reads and receiving nearly ten thousand reader letters. He then founded *Weekly Review*, creating opinion influence on campus. At Penn, he founded "North America Three," which within three years grew into one of the most influential new-media platforms in the North American Chinese-student community.
These aren't random events. They're repeated displays of the same ability: **packaging cognition into narrative, then turning narrative into influence**.
In the crypto domain, this ability was pushed to the extreme. TRON's white paper was accused of plagiarizing Ethereum and IPFS—Ethereum founder Vitalik Buterin even mocked that "TRON's efficiency at copy-pasting white papers far exceeds its originality." But ironically, TRON's ICO still blew up, selling out in 53 seconds. Because what Justin Sun sold wasn't technology, it was narrative. He sold the story-combination of "post-90s entrepreneur + blockchain + decentralized entertainment + the future."
Narrative ability is a cognitive accelerator because it forms a positive feedback loop: the clearer your cognition, the more persuasive your narrative → the more persuasive your narrative, the more resources you attract → the more resources, the denser the information you access → the denser the information, the clearer your cognition. Once this loop starts, it self-accelerates.
Its limitation is equally obvious: narrative ability can amplify cognition, and it can amplify error. When a person's narrative ability far exceeds their cognitive depth, they become an efficient "bullshitter"—and they themselves may not be able to tell whether they truly understand or are just bullshitting. Li Xiao's evaluation of Justin Sun as "definitely bullshitting" may be too simple, but it points to a real risk.

### Accelerator Four: Asymmetric Risk Preference

Prospect theory in behavioral economics tells us: people are about twice as sensitive to losses as to gains. This means a 40-year-old with a family, a mortgage, and professional reputation will have far higher "loss aversion" in decision-making than a 23-year-old student.
When the 23-year-old Justin Sun bought bitcoin with his tuition, the risk structure of that decision was highly asymmetric: the worst case was losing a few tens of thousands in tuition—for a student with no family burden, not a fatal loss; the best case was a return of dozens of times. Losses had a floor, gains had no ceiling.
The same decision in front of a 40-year-old has a completely different structure: he must consider family expenses, children's education, retirement savings, professional reputation. Even if his judgment were as strong as Justin Sun's, his action space is much smaller.
This implies a counterintuitive conclusion: **youth itself is a cognitive advantage**—not because the young are smarter, but because the young have a flatter loss function. They can make more high-variance attempts, and each attempt brings cognitive feedback, win or lose. A person who has tried investing, founding companies, writing, and public speaking by 23 will, by 30, have a cognitive experience library far exceeding someone who walked the conventional path over the same time.
But the dark side of this accelerator: low loss aversion not only makes people dare to take smart risks, it also makes them dare to take reckless ones. The controversies surrounding Justin Sun—from plagiarism accusations over TRON's white paper, to repeatedly reneging on the Buffett lunch, to being called a "crypto huckster"—can largely be traced to this low loss aversion: when you're not sensitive enough to reputational loss, you make moves "ordinary people wouldn't dare make," sometimes acts of genius, sometimes ethical controversies.

### The Cross-Effect of the Four Accelerators

These four accelerators don't operate independently; there's a strong cross-amplification effect among them.
Circle leaps provide high-density information → action within high-density information brings high-frequency feedback → cognition accumulated from high-frequency feedback is monetized into resources and influence through narrative ability → resources and influence in turn help entry into higher circles. And asymmetric risk preference is the lubricant of this whole cycle—it lowers the startup cost of every step.
When these four accelerators run simultaneously, a person's cognitive accumulation in five years can equal twenty or even thirty years on the conventional path. This is why Justin Sun's judgment at 23 could, on certain dimensions, surpass that of most 40-year-olds.
But precisely because all components of this system point to "winning"—spotting opportunities, acting fast, packaging narrative, daring to risk—it produced extreme cognitive acceleration on the dimension of "winning," while leaving significant gaps on other dimensions.

## The Brain Isn't Fully Assembled? A Popular Misconception

Neuroscience has a widely circulated claim: a person's prefrontal cortex doesn't fully mature until 25. The prefrontal cortex is responsible for exactly decision-making, risk assessment, and long-term planning—these higher cognitive functions. By this logic, the 23-year-old Justin Sun shouldn't have had the judgment he displayed—his brain hardware wasn't assembled yet.
But this claim is itself an oversimplified myth.
Research published in Nature Communications in 2025 shows that prefrontal cortex development actually extends into the thirties, far beyond the previously assumed 25 node. More importantly, Wikipedia has set up a separate "myth" entry for "the brain matures at 25"—neuroscientist Jay Giedd's original research was repeatedly simplified and misread by mass media, eventually becoming an inaccurate popular saying.
The real situation is subtler: different brain regions mature at different rates. The limbic system, responsible for emotional response, is basically mature by around age 12, while the prefrontal cortex, responsible for impulse inhibition and long-term planning, matures latest. This means young people are indeed more impulsive and more susceptible to peer influence—but that doesn't mean they can't make high-quality decisions.
The key distinction: **making high-quality decisions doesn't require a fully mature prefrontal cortex, but a specific type of cognitive training**.

## The Dimensions of Cognition Are Uneven

Developmental psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg made an important insight in his theory of moral development: moral reasoning ability and intellectual development are two independent dimensions. A person can perform excellently on IQ tests while remaining at a lower developmental stage in moral reasoning.
This framework can be extended to other dimensions of cognition. Cognition isn't a single ability—it contains at least several relatively independent dimensions:

- **Game-theoretic cognition**: the ability to understand interest structures, recognize opportunity windows, and anticipate opponents' behavior
- **Systems cognition**: the ability to understand the operating logic of complex systems, recognize causal chains and feedback loops
- **Moral cognition**: the ability to evaluate the ethical consequences of behavior and weigh the interests of different stakeholders
- **Existential cognition**: the ability to understand life's finitude, meaning, and long-term value

These dimensions mature at different rates. Game-theoretic cognition can iterate fast through high-density market feedback—because the market's feedback to you is immediate, quantitative, and merciless. Systems cognition needs a longer observation cycle. Moral cognition takes the longest—because the ethical consequences of many decisions take years or even decades to manifest.
Justin Sun's case perfectly displays this uneven development.
His game-theoretic cognition had already reached an extremely high level at 23: the Tian-Ji-style transfer strategy, precise grasp of crypto market timing, the marketing calculation of the Buffett lunch—each displayed game-theoretic intuition far beyond his peers.
But his moral cognition sparked wide controversy. Li Xiao called him "definitely bullshitting." Sohu CEO Charles Zhang called him a "swindler." Xpeng board chairman He Xiaopeng called him "shamelessly running scams to fleece the market." In 2023, the US SEC filed a market-manipulation lawsuit against him. He was nicknamed "crypto huckster" and "Sun the harvester."
These controversies aren't accidental. They point precisely to the uneven development of cognition: a person far ahead of peers on the game-theoretic dimension may not have matured in sync on the moral dimension.

### Why "Winning Cognition" Can Mature Early While "Good Cognition" Lags

The reason lies in the fundamentally different feedback structures of the two dimensions.
The feedback for game-theoretic cognition is immediate. You make an investment decision and know within days or weeks whether you were right or wrong. You launch a marketing campaign and see traffic data within hours. This immediate feedback lets game-theoretic cognition iterate fast—a 23-year-old who began operating densely in markets at 18 can, within five years, accumulate game-theoretic experience that takes ordinary people twenty years.
The feedback for moral cognition is delayed. You make a decision that harms others' trust, and short-term it may look like a win—market cap rose, traffic came, fame grew. But the real cost of that decision may surface only five or ten years later: partners no longer trust you, your industry reputation keeps deteriorating, legal risk accumulates to a point that can't be ignored.
Justin Sun's own line again provides a precise footnote: "Eating dirt is ugly, but there's no other way." This is a classic short-feedback-cycle judgment—in the current game, "ugly dirt-eating" yields benefits exceeding the cost. But if you stretch the time scale to ten or twenty years, does this judgment still hold?
TRON's white paper was accused of plagiarism, with near-zero short-term impact—the ICO still sold out in 53 seconds. But long-term, this controversy became a standard paragraph in every report about Justin Sun, following him forever. The repeated welshing on the Buffett lunch generated short-term buzz, but long-term consumed his credibility in the traditional finance world. The SEC's market-manipulation lawsuit is a classic delayed feedback—the behavior happened years earlier, the legal consequences arrived years later.
This isn't a moral trial. It's pointing out a structural fact: **different dimensions of cognition mature at different rates, and this unevenness is itself the most important feature of cognitive precocity**.
Those who display astonishing game-theoretic ability young are often also hotspots of moral controversy. Not because they're "bad people," but because their cognitive system runs too fast on one dimension while others haven't caught up.

## What This Means for You

If you're not Justin Sun—most people aren't, and don't need to be—what does this analysis mean for you?
Three judgment frameworks you can use immediately:
**First, actively enter high-information-density environments.**
Your cognitive ceiling is to a large extent determined by the information environment you're in. If the information you encounter every day is from the same circle and the same dimension, your cognitive iteration speed will be very slow. Actively enter an environment that makes you feel "a bit out of your depth": attend meetings you're not quite qualified for, read papers you can't quite understand, talk with people far more experienced than you. Discomfort is a signal that information density is rising.
But be careful to distinguish "information density" from "information noise." Scrolling social media isn't high information density, it's high information noise. The truly high-density environments are those that force you to process a lot of structured information in a short time: real investing, participating in early projects, writing articles that need tight argumentation.
**Second, shorten the delay between "thinking" and "doing."**
Most people's cognitive bottleneck isn't "can't think of it," it's "thought of it but didn't do it." Every action is a cognitive experiment, and every experiment brings feedback you can't get from pure thinking. A person who has run ten decision experiments by 23 has far higher cognitive quality than one who has only thought a hundred times by 23 but never acted.
This isn't to say "don't think, just act"—it's to say, when you have a judgment you're 70% sure of, do it, rather than waiting until 90%. That extra 20% of certainty is gained far faster through action than through thinking.
**Third, distinguish "winning cognition" from "right cognition."**
This is the most important one. Justin Sun's case tells us the different dimensions of cognition can develop independently. A person can be far ahead of peers on game-theoretic cognition while seriously lagging on moral or systems cognition. Realizing this, you won't blindly worship any "cognitively precocious" figure—they do see farther on some dimension, but may see less far than you on others.
Likewise, examine your own cognitive structure: are you running fast on some dimension but under-accumulated on others? Has your cognitive system over-optimized one objective function—income, status, efficiency—while neglecting dimensions with longer feedback cycles but equal importance?

Back to that opening scene. In 2008, a PKU Chinese-department student decided to transfer down into the history department. He'd done the Tian-Ji-racing arithmetic: ranking first on a weak track is more useful than ranking middle on a strong one.
That judgment was right. His later trajectory proved it—first in his year, admission to five Ivy schools, Ripple China representative, TRON founder, no. 411 on the Forbes global billionaires list. From the "winning" dimension, this is a textbook case of cognitive precocity.
But the Tian-Ji logic has a blind spot it can't see itself: it always asks "how to win this match," and never asks "is this match worth winning."
Truly profound cognition is not knowing how to win any given match.
It's knowing when to switch to a different match.
